The lapel pin exists because a buttonhole was left over. When men's jackets kept a working buttonhole on the left lapel long after anyone stopped buttoning it, a small hole sat there with no job. Nature abhors an idle buttonhole. So people put flowers in it, then ribbons, then little enamelled facts about themselves.

That is the entire origin story. A vestigial feature that refused to be useless.

By the twentieth century the pin had learned to talk. Trade unions used them. Political campaigns used them. Fraternal orders with alarming numbers of secret handshakes used them constantly. A pin was a way of saying *I belong to this* without having to explain, over drinks, what *this* was.

What we like about that history is how little of it was necessary. No pin ever kept anyone warm or fed. A pin is pure declaration, a badge for an allegiance you chose, worn at roughly heart height, small enough to deny if the room turns hostile.

We make ours in that spirit. Not to be useful. To be kept. The buttonhole is still there, waiting, on more jackets than you'd think.
